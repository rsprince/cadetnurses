Cadet Nurse Narrative Analysis Report

Initial analysis of career and leadership information contained in profile narratives

Overview

This report analyzes all 332 records identified as Cadet Nurse profiles in the website data export. The analysis relies on information found in the narrative text of each profile rather than only on existing structured database fields.

Key interpretation: The results below represent explicit evidence found in the narratives. They should be treated as conservative minimums because many profiles contain little or no information about the nurse's later career.

Main Findings

Finding Profiles Percentage
Explicit evidence of working in nursing after training 170 51.2%
Insufficient career information in the narrative 162 48.8%
Explicit evidence of a leadership role 24 7.2%
Leadership among profiles with documented nursing careers 24 of 170 14.1%
At least 51% of the profiles explicitly document that the Cadet Nurse continued working in nursing.

This is a conservative minimum. It does not mean that only 51% remained in nursing. Nearly half of the narratives simply do not provide enough post-training career information to answer the question.

At least 7% of all profiles, and 14% of profiles with documented nursing careers, explicitly identify a leadership position.

Leadership Positions Identified

The named leadership roles found in the narratives included:

  • Director of Nursing or Director of Nurses
  • Head Nurse
  • Charge Nurse
  • Nursing Supervisor
  • Nurse Manager
  • Hospital or nursing-home administrator
  • Director of a university nursing school
  • Executive or program leadership roles

Examples include Dorothy Harrington Hall, Mae F. Keefer, Marian Marcella McRaith Tope, Phyllis Noerager Stern, Georgia Anne Scott Poplar, Miriam Weissman Sprince, and others.

Important Limitation

“No evidence found” does not mean the nurse never held such a position. Many profiles contain only training dates, school information, a death notice, or a few sentences about Cadet Nurse service. Leadership and later-career information may simply have been omitted.

Leadership was not inferred merely because someone was described as a professor, instructor, senior nurse, or long-term practitioner. The narrative needed to identify a reasonably explicit leadership role.

Conclusion

This is a useful first-pass dataset, but the 162 profiles with insufficient career information are the central limitation. They should remain classified as unknown rather than being counted as nurses who left the profession. That distinction is essential to preserving the historical accuracy of the analysis.
